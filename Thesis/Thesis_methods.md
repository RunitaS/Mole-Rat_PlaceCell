### Single unit criteria
#### l-ratio and isolation distance calc.

Cluster quality was quantified for every semi-manually sorted single unit using Isolation Distance (Harris et al., 2001) and L-ratio (Schmitzer-Torbert et al., 2005), computed directly from the raw tetrode waveforms recorded in Neuralynx .ntt files, following the reference implementation in the mclust toolbox (Redish; `IsolationDistance.m`, `L_Ratio.m`).

**Feature space**: For each tetrode, a feature matrix was built from the 32-sample, 4-channel waveform of every recorded spike (sorted and unsorted), reproducing mclust's default `ClusterSeparationFeatures` set {Energy, WavePC1} rather than a PCA on raw waveform samples. For each of the 4 channels, two features were computed per spike: (1) Energy, the per-channel waveform L2 norm normalized by the square root of the sample count; and (2) WavePC1, obtained by first unit-L2-normalizing each spike's waveform on that channel (removing amplitude/energy information so only waveform shape remains), then projecting the resulting shape vectors onto their first principal component — with the PCA performed on the *correlation* matrix (not the raw covariance matrix) across spikes so that each of the 32 time samples contributes equally regardless of its variance. Concatenating Energy and WavePC1 across all 4 channels gave an 8-dimensional feature vector (FD) per spike.

**Mahalanobis distance**: For a given cluster, the squared Mahalanobis distance of every spike in the tetrode (cluster and non-cluster) to that cluster was computed using the mean and covariance of the cluster's own spikes only in feature space (not the pooled covariance of all spikes), matching MATLAB's `mahal(FD, FD_cluster)` convention.

**Isolation Distance**: Measures quality of separation from rest of clusters. defined as the squared Mahalanobis distance of the N_C-th closest non-cluster ("noise") spike to the cluster, where N_C is the number of spikes in the cluster; undefined (NaN) when the cluster contains more than half of all recorded spikes on that tetrode. Larger values indicate a cluster more separated from the rest of the recorded spikes on that tetrode.

**L-ratio**: measures contamination from other clusters. for each non-cluster spike, its squared Mahalanobis distance to the cluster was converted to a tail probability under a χ² distribution with degrees of freedom equal to the dimensionality of the feature space (df = 8), using 1 − χ²cdf(d², df); L-ratio is the sum of this quantity across all non-cluster spikes, divided by the number of spikes in the cluster. Smaller values indicate a cluster better separated from surrounding spikes.

**Shuffle-based significance test**: To test whether a cluster's separation exceeded what would be expected from the most confusable background spikes on that tetrode (rather than from the full, largely dissimilar spike population), a nearest-neighbour shuffle null was constructed per cluster. The non-cluster spikes were ranked by their Mahalanobis distance to the real cluster, and the 3×N_C nearest ("most confusable") spikes formed a neighbour pool. On each of 500 shuffles, a pseudo-cluster of N_C spikes was drawn without replacement from this pool, and L-ratio and Isolation Distance were recomputed treating the pseudo-cluster as if it were the real cluster, yielding a null distribution for each metric. One-tailed empirical p-values were obtained as the (add-one-smoothed) proportion of null L-ratio values less than or equal to, and null Isolation Distance values greater than or equal to, the real cluster's values; a z-score of the real value against the null mean/SD was computed in the same direction for each metric. A cluster was considered significantly separated from its most confusable neighbours when both p-values were below 0.05.

#### SNR and waveform template quality

To complement the L-ratio/Isolation Distance criteria above, each unit was additionally screened using signal-to-noise ratio, refractory-period violations, and waveform template consistency, computed directly from the raw 32-sample, 4-channel waveforms in the tetrode's Neuralynx .ntt file.

**Signal-to-noise ratio (SNR)**: The channel and sample of the unit's largest deflection was identified from its mean waveform, and the noise floor on that channel was estimated as the median absolute deviation (MAD) of the pre-spike baseline (first 5 samples of every spike's waveform, pooled across spikes), scaled by 1/0.6745 to give a normal-equivalent baseline standard deviation (σ_noise). SNR was defined as A_peak/σ_noise (equivalently, 20·log10(SNR) in dB). Units were classified as too low (SNR < 1.5), marginal (1.5 ≤ SNR < 2.5), good (2.5 ≤ SNR ≤ 4.0), or very high (SNR > 4.0).

**Inter-spike-interval (ISI) violations**: For each unit, spike timestamps were sorted and the percentage of consecutive ISIs shorter than a 1 ms refractory period was calculated; units were accepted only if the violation rate was below 1%.

**Waveform template correlation**: A mean waveform template was computed per unit by averaging its 32-sample, 4-channel waveforms across all spikes. Each individual spike's waveform was demeaned per channel and correlated (Pearson r) against the equivalently demeaned template; this correlation was averaged first across the unit's spikes and then across the four tetrode channels, giving a single mean template-correlation value per unit that indexes waveform consistency across the recording.

#### spike duration and PTT ratio

**Channel and waveform selection**: For each unit, the mean waveform (averaged across all spikes) was computed separately for each of the tetrode's four channels. The channel whose mean waveform reached the largest positive peak amplitude (not the largest peak-to-peak spread) was selected as the reference channel for all subsequent waveform-shape measurements.

**Peak-to-trough (PT) time and ratio**: On the reference channel's mean waveform, the sample indices of the maximum (peak) and minimum (trough) voltage were identified. PT time was calculated as the absolute time separation between these two samples, in ms, using the waveform's known 5 ms/32-sample sampling interval. The PT amplitude ratio was calculated as |peak amplitude| / |trough amplitude|.

**Spike duration**: Spike duration was defined as the interval from the onset of depolarization to the end of the after-hyperpolarization on the reference channel's mean waveform. A baseline mean and a MAD-based baseline σ were estimated from the first 5 samples of the waveform (the same pre-spike window used for the SNR calculation above). A **threshold of 2 baseline-σ around the baseline mean was used to locate, moving backward in time from the earlier of the peak/trough**, the last sample within this threshold (onset of depolarization), and, moving forward in time from the later of the peak/trough, the first sample within this threshold (end of after-hyperpolarization). Spike duration was the elapsed time between the onset and offset samples.

#### complex spiking index calc

**Complex Spike Index (CSI)**: Following Ranck (1973) and Bhatt et al. (2020), a complex spike burst was defined as a pair of consecutive spikes separated by an ISI of 3–20 ms in which the second spike's amplitude was smaller than the first. For each unit, spike timestamps were sorted and all consecutive ISIs were computed; the per-spike peak-to-peak amplitude was taken as the mean, across the tetrode's four channels, of that spike's (max − min) waveform value. CSI was defined as the percentage of all consecutive ISIs for the unit that both fell within the 3–20 ms window and showed this amplitude decrement: CSI = 100 × (number of qualifying spike pairs) / (total number of ISIs).

### Place cell criteria

#### Place cell characterization

For every isolated single unit, an occupancy-weighted rate map was built from the same tracking/spike pipeline used throughout (see Place field criteria and Mean rate map below): the arena was discretized into 2 × 2 cm bins, spikes were assigned to the nearest tracking frame within a 50 ms gate, per-bin occupancy was accumulated as summed inter-frame duration (the first frame credited 1/fps, and any inter-frame gap capped at 2/fps to prevent tracking dropouts from inflating occupancy), and bins with less than 1 s of total occupancy were excluded from every metric below ("valid" bins). The raw rate per bin (spike count ÷ occupancy) was smoothed with a 3 × 3 triangular kernel (weights 1-2-1 per row/column, normalized to sum to 1), convolved separately over the zero-filled raw-rate map and over the binary valid-bin mask, with the smoothed rate at each bin taken as the ratio of the two convolutions — correcting for the reduced neighbour support at the edges of the visited arena. Unless stated otherwise, "rate map" below refers to this occupancy-weighted, triangularly smoothed map, and for each valid bin i, p_i and r_i denote its fractional occupancy (occupancy_i ÷ total occupied time) and its smoothed firing rate.

**Peak firing rate**: the maximum smoothed firing rate (Hz) across all valid bins of the full-session rate map.

**Spatial information score**: spatial information rate (SIR, bits/spike) was computed following Skaggs, McNaughton & Gothard (1993): SIR = Σ_i p_i (r_i / r̄) log2(r_i / r̄), summed over valid bins with r_i > 0, where r̄ = Σ_i p_i r_i is the cell's occupancy-weighted mean firing rate.

**Sparsity score**: spatial sparsity followed Skaggs, McNaughton, Markus & Gothard (1996): sparsity = (Σ_i p_i r_i)² / Σ_i p_i r_i², which approaches 0 when firing is concentrated in a small fraction of the arena and 1 when firing is uniform across all visited bins.

**Coherence score**: spatial coherence (Muller & Kubie, 1989) was computed from the raw (unsmoothed), occupancy-normalized rate map rather than the smoothed map used for the other metrics: for every valid bin, its own raw rate was paired with the mean raw rate of its valid 8-connected (Moore) neighbours, and the Pearson correlation between the two resulting vectors — taken over all valid bins with at least one valid neighbour — was Fisher z-transformed (z = 0.5·ln[(1+r)/(1−r)], r clipped to ±0.9999 to avoid infinities) to give the reported coherence score.

**Stability score**: within-session reliability of the rate map was quantified as the Pearson correlation, restricted to bins valid in both maps being compared, between independently built rate maps from different temporal subsets of the same session. The session's tracking frames, sorted by time, were split by sample count into a first and second half and into four equal quarters (Q1–Q4); each subset's rate map was built with the identical binning, occupancy, and triangular-smoothing procedure as the full-session map, using only the position/spike samples falling in that subset. Stability was reported separately for each of seven map-pair comparisons: full session vs. first half, full session vs. second half, first half vs. second half, Q1 vs. Q2, Q3 vs. Q4, Q1 vs. Q3, and Q2 vs. Q4. A comparison was reported as undefined (rather than a correlation coefficient) when fewer than 3 bins were valid in both maps of the pair, or when either map's valid-bin rates had zero variance.

##### speed modulation and speed cell

Whether a unit's firing rate scaled with the animal's running speed was assessed once per unit, from the full session only (not from the first/second-half or quartile splits used for stability above), using two complementary measures — a binned firing-rate-vs-speed regression and a time-domain (sample-by-sample) correlation — each carried through its own circular-shift shuffle significance test.

**Speed and instantaneous firing rate**: position tracking was first cleaned and smoothed as follows, independent of and in addition to the ratemap-binning pipeline above: frame-to-frame steps implying a speed above 80 cm/s were iteratively flagged as tracking artifacts (re-tested against the shrinking set of "good" frames so runs of consecutive bad frames were caught, not just single-frame jumps), the flagged frames were replaced by linear interpolation between the nearest surviving good frames, and the resulting x/y trace was Gaussian-smoothed (σ = 1 sample). This cleaned trace was then downsampled by a factor of 3 (30 → 15 fps, keeping every third frame) specifically for the speed analysis, because at the native 33.3 ms frame spacing the speed-vs-rate cloud was too scattered; the ratemap/SIR/sparsity/coherence/stability metrics above are unaffected and still use the native-rate tracking. On this downsampled track, per-frame running speed (cm/s) was calculated as the Euclidean frame-to-frame displacement divided by the true (timestamp-derived, not assumed-fixed) inter-frame interval, and instantaneous firing rate was calculated on the same frame base as spike count per inter-frame interval ÷ interval duration, using the same 50 ms spike-to-frame gate as the ratemap. Both the speed trace and the instantaneous-rate trace were then Gaussian-smoothed with the same kernel (σ = 0.3 s, expressed in samples at the downsampled 15 fps rate) so that the two signals being correlated were smoothed on equal footing. Samples were retained for the analyses below only if their smoothed speed fell strictly between 2 and 80 cm/s, their smoothed rate was finite, and their raw (unsmoothed) instantaneous rate was nonzero (i.e., at least one spike fell in that interval) — excluding near-stationary and tracking-artifact speeds, and excluding silent intervals that would otherwise dilute the correlation toward zero without being informative about the rate-speed relationship.

**Binned speed score**: following the MATLAB `speed_firing_runita` reference method, the retained samples were binned into 4 cm/s-wide speed bins spanning 2–80 cm/s, each bin's representative firing rate was taken as the median (not mean) of its member samples' smoothed rate — reducing the influence of an occasional high-rate burst within an otherwise low-rate bin — and bins holding less than 0.2% of all retained samples (chiefly the sparsely sampled high-speed tail) were discarded. A weighted least-squares regression of binned median rate against bin-centre speed (rate = β·speed + f0) was then fit across the surviving bins, each bin weighted by its own sample count so that sparse bins could not have the same leverage on the fit as densely sampled ones; the weighted Pearson correlation coefficient from this fit is the reported binned speed score, and its two-tailed significance was tested parametrically via a t-test on the regression slope (df = n bins − 2).

**Time-domain speed score (Iwase et al., 2020)**: independently of the binning step above, a second speed score was computed as the direct, sample-by-sample Pearson correlation between the smoothed speed and smoothed firing-rate traces across all retained samples, with no speed-binning. This is the measure used for the paper's speed-cell classification, below; the accompanying parametric p-value is reported for reference only; it assumes independent residuals, an assumption violated by the shared Gaussian smoothing, and so is anti-conservatively biased — the shuffle-based significance test below, not this parametric p-value, was used to judge significance.

**Circular-shift shuffle significance**: for each of the two scores above, a null distribution was built by circularly shifting the per-interval spike-count trace by a random offset of at least 20 s from either end of the session (the same shift-margin convention as the spatial-information shuffle), recomputing the instantaneous rate, its Gaussian smoothing, and the corresponding score (binned weighted-regression r, or time-domain Pearson r) from the shifted trace while leaving the (unshifted) speed trace, bin membership, and per-bin weights untouched, and repeating this 1000 times. As a compute-saving screen, a method's shuffle was only run if that method's own parametric fit was already nominally significant (binned regression: p < 0.05 on the slope; time-domain: p < 0.05 on the raw Pearson correlation); a method that failed this initial screen was left unclassified for significance rather than shuffled. From each surviving null distribution, a two-tailed 95% shuffle confidence interval (2.5th/97.5th percentiles) and a two-tailed, signed-tail, add-one-smoothed empirical p-value were computed, and a unit was classified as significantly speed-modulated by that method when its real score fell outside the 95% shuffle CI (equivalently, shuffle p < 0.05).

**Speed-cell classification (p-Speed / n-Speed)**: following the positive/negative speed-cell convention of Iwase et al. (2020), a unit was classified as a positive speed cell (p-Speed) if its real time-domain speed score exceeded the upper bound of its own two-tailed 95% shuffle confidence interval, and as a negative speed cell (n-Speed) if it fell below the lower bound; units whose time-domain score fell inside the shuffle CI were classified as non-speed cells. This departs from the original one-sided 1st/99th-percentile criterion of Iwase et al. by using the same two-tailed 2.5th/97.5th-percentile shuffle CI applied to the binned speed score above, so that both measures are judged against a CI built the same way.

#### Place field criteria

Individual place fields were isolated from each qualifying place cell's rate map (occupancy-weighted, triangularly smoothed, 2 × 2 cm bins, spikes matched to the nearest tracking frame within 50 ms, bins with <1 s occupancy excluded — as above) using a single-pass, non-iterative "threshold method" adapted from a MATLAB `placefield`/`getLegals` reference implementation.

**Qualifying bins**: A bin was included in a candidate field only if its smoothed firing rate was both ≥10% of the cell's own peak (smoothed) rate and above the cell's mean firing rate — the dual criterion excluding both low-rate bins at the field's periphery and bins that clear the peak-relative cutoff yet remain below the cell's overall average activity.

**Connected-component labelling**: Qualifying bins were grouped by 8-connected-component (diagonal-inclusive) flood fill, directly mirroring the visited/`getLegals` flood-fill approach of the MATLAB reference; each connected region of qualifying bins was evaluated independently, with no iterative re-bootstrapping or peak-suppression step.

**Field size criterion**: A candidate region was accepted as a place field only if it spanned at least 4% of the cell's total occupied (visited) bins (rounded up to a whole bin); smaller regions were discarded as noise-level fluctuations rather than genuine fields.

**Field statistics**: For each accepted field, the peak bin (maximum smoothed firing rate within the region) and the firing-rate-weighted centre of mass were computed, mirroring the reference implementation's `fieldPos`/`centreFieldSize`. Field extent was converted to physical units (area in cm², percentage of the cell's occupied arena) and each field's bounding box (bin and cm coordinates) was retained. Where a cell had more than one accepted field, the field whose centre of mass fell nearest the arena's geometric centre was additionally flagged as the cell's "centre field".

**Reported metrics**: number of fields per cell, area (cm²) and percentage of occupied arena covered per field, and total field area/coverage summed across all of a cell's fields.

### Mean rate map

Overall mean firing-rate maps (Fig. S1H) and their quadrant-folded counterparts (Fig. 1B/D) were computed by pooling all qualifying place cells recorded across days and sessions within each arena, following the quadrant-registration method of Muessig et al. (Fig. 1A).

**Tracking preprocessing**: For each session, x/y tracking on the same time base as spike timestamps was first coarse-filtered by discarding untracked frames (coordinate value ±1) and any frame-to-frame step whose raw displacement/time ratio implied an implausible jump, then converted from pixels to cm using a per-session pixel-to-cm scale factor (the larger of the tracked x- or y-extent divided by the arena's physical width; skipped when tracking was already recorded in cm). On the resulting cm trace, an iterative jump-removal step flagged and linearly interpolated over any further frame-to-frame step implying speed > 80 cm/s, and the cleaned x/y trace was then Gaussian-smoothed (σ = 1 sample).

**Rate-map construction**: Each arena's spatial extent was discretized into ~2 × 2 cm bins (or the along-track/across-track equivalent — see arena geometries below), and every position sample was assigned a single flat bin index. Spikes were matched to the nearest tracking frame by timestamp, with a match discarded if the nearest frame was more than 50 ms away. Per-bin occupancy was accumulated as summed inter-frame duration (the first frame credited 1/fps, and any inter-frame gap capped at 2/fps so that tracking dropouts did not inflate occupancy), and bins with less than 1 s of total occupancy, or lying outside the arena's physical geometry, were excluded. Raw firing rate per bin was spike count ÷ occupancy, and this raw map was smoothed with an occupancy-weighted triangular kernel appropriate to the arena's topology: a 3 × 3 kernel (weights 1-2-1 per row/column, normalized) for the genuinely 2D open field and linear track, and a 3-tap 1D kernel (1-2-1, normalized) applied circularly (wrap-around, no edge effect) for the 1D circular track ring.

**Place-cell qualification**: For each isolated unit, spatial information rate (SIR, in bits/spike) and sparsity were computed from the occupancy-weighted, smoothed rate map following the standard Skaggs formulation, and compared against a location-shuffle null: on each of 1000 shuffles, every spike's assigned tracking frame was shifted by the same random circular offset (drawn with a 20 s margin from either end of the session to avoid trivial self-alignment), the rate map and SIR were recomputed from the shuffled spike-frame assignment, and the unit's real SIR was compared to the resulting null distribution's 95th percentile (sessions shorter than 40 s could not be shuffled and were left unclassified). A unit was accepted as a place cell only if its real SIR exceeded this shuffle threshold, it fired more than 50 spikes, its peak (smoothed) rate fell between 1 and 15 Hz, its SIR exceeded 0.5 bits/spike, and its sparsity was below 0.9.

**Arena geometries**:
- *Open field* (60 cm diameter circle): square 2 × 2 cm bins over a grid spanning the diameter; a bin was counted toward final occupancy/geometry only if its center fell within the arena radius, while a slightly wider radius (+1 bin) was used when assigning individual position samples to bins.
- *Circular track* (72 cm inner / 80 cm outer diameter): position was re-expressed as radius and angle about the arena center; samples were accepted as "on-track" within the annulus ± 4 cm radial tolerance, and the ring was divided into angular bins sized to ~2 cm of arc length at the ring's mean radius, rounded to the nearest multiple of 4 bins so the ring folds into exactly four equal arcs for the quadrant analysis below.
- *Linear track* (80 × 8 cm): binned genuinely in 2D — along the 80 cm length and across the 8 cm width, each in ~2 cm bins — rather than collapsed to a single row across the width. Sessions in which the track's long axis ran along the tracked y-extent (rather than x) were rotated 90° so every session pools onto the same length axis regardless of the track's physical orientation in the room.

**Fig. S1H — overall mean rate map**: For each arena, the per-bin raw (unsmoothed) firing rate of every qualifying place cell was averaged bin-by-bin across cells (a bin missing occupancy for a given cell was excluded from that bin's average rather than treated as zero), giving one fine-resolution mean rate map per arena, plotted in Cartesian view for the open field and linear track and in polar view for the circular track.

**Quadrant folding (Fig. 1A method)**: The whole-arena bin grid was cut into four regions and each was registered into a single reference region's local bin coordinates, so that a bin-by-bin average across the four registered copies could be taken. For the open field and linear track, this registration was done by mirror-reflecting each axis about its own midpoint independently (rather than by a 90° rotation of the whole grid, which would swap the two axes and cross-match, e.g., a track-length wall against a track-width wall for two of the four quadrants), yielding one reference quadrant of half-width × half-height bins in which every quadrant's "wall a" always maps onto the reference's "wall a" and "wall b" onto "wall b". The circular track has no distinct walls to register against one another, so it was instead split into four equal 90° arcs, each folded onto one reference arc while preserving its internal along-track bin structure.

**Fig. 1B — folded peak-location proportion**: Each qualifying place cell contributed the single bin of its own peak (smoothed) firing rate, mapped into the corresponding bin of the reference quadrant/arc; because a peak is a single location it was not itself averaged across the four folded regions. The proportion of all cells' peaks falling in each folded bin (%) is reported.

**Fig. 1D — folded mean rate map**: The overall (unsmoothed) mean rate map from Fig. S1H was folded through the same quadrant/arc mapping and averaged bin-by-bin across the four folded regions, giving one small quadrant mean-firing-rate map per arena.

**Output**: Colour scales (jet colormap) were normalized per figure to the min/max of that figure's pooled valid bin values. Per-unit summary metrics (spike count, peak and mean firing rate, SIR, sparsity, bootstrap significance, place-cell classification, and folded peak-quadrant bin) were exported to a summary table alongside the figures.

### Edge vs Center zone

To test whether place cells preferentially represented the boundary or the interior of an arena, every qualifying place cell (see Place cell characterization above) was assigned to an "edge" or "centre" zone from the location of its own peak-firing bin, and firing/spatial-coding properties were then compared between the resulting groups. Classification used the same occupancy-weighted, triangularly smoothed 2×2 cm rate map built for the place-cell characterization above (bins with <1 s occupancy excluded), run independently for the open field and linear track.

**Peak-bin localization**: For each unit, the single visited bin with the highest smoothed firing rate was located in that unit's full-session rate map (the half-session/quartile maps used only for stability, above, were not classified), and the (x,y) coordinates of that bin's centre were converted into a distance from the nearest arena boundary using the arena-specific geometry below.

**Open field (60 cm diameter circle)**: distance from the wall was defined as the arena radius (30 cm) minus the peak bin's distance from the arena centre. A cell was classified "edge" if this distance was ≤9.25 cm, and "centre" otherwise.

**Linear track (80 × 8 cm)**: the track's running axis was inferred per session from the longer of the tracked x/y extents. Two distances were computed for the peak bin: its distance from the track's mid-line across the short (width) axis, and its distance from the nearer of the two short end walls along the long (length) axis. A cell was classified "edge" if its peak bin fell within 2 cm of either long (side) wall (i.e. ≥2 cm from the mid-line, since the track's half-width is 4 cm) or within 2 cm of either short end wall, and "centre" otherwise (within 2 cm of the mid-line and more than 2 cm from both ends). The reported dist_from_wall_cm was the smaller of the distance to the nearest long wall and the distance to the nearest end wall.

**Session-level occupancy split**: independent of any individual cell's firing, the same zone boundaries were applied to every visited bin of each session's occupancy-time map to compute the proportion of the session's total tracked time spent in the edge versus centre zone, visualized as a 2D occupancy heat map with the edge/centre boundary overlaid (dashed circle for the open field; dashed lines parallel to the mid-line and end walls for the linear track).

**Zone comparison statistics**: Restricting to units classified as place cells, dist_from_wall_cm was compared between edge- and centre-zone cells with a two-tailed Welch's t-test (unequal variances). The same edge/centre grouping was used to compare five rate-map metrics — peak firing rate, mean firing rate, spatial information rate, sparsity, and coherence — between zones with a two-sided Mann–Whitney U test, run separately per arena and requiring at least 2 cells per zone.

**Plots**: For each arena, the edge-vs-centre distributions of dist_from_wall_cm and of each of the five metrics above were plotted as box-and-jitter plots (median, IQR, individual place cells overlaid as jittered points), annotated with the Mann–Whitney U statistic, p-value, and significance stars (*** p<0.001, ** p<0.01, * p<0.05, ns otherwise). Where boundary analysis was run for more than one arena, the proportion of edge versus centre place cells was additionally compared across arenas with a stacked bar chart.

#### PSD plot using FOOOF analysis

Power spectra were parameterized into periodic (oscillatory) and aperiodic (1/f) components using FOOOF/specparam (Donoghue et al., 2020; `fooof` Python package, v1.1.1), separately for each animal.

**Signal preprocessing**: Raw LFP (Neuralynx .ncs, 32 kHz, converted to µV using the Neuralynx ADBitVolts scale factor) was downsampled to 500 Hz (`scipy.signal.resample_poly`), 50 Hz mains noise and its harmonics (50, 100, 150, 200 Hz) were removed with zero-phase IIR notch filters (Q = 30), and the resulting trace was linearly detrended to remove slow drift not addressed by the notch filter.

**Epoching and three-stage epoch rejection**: The cleaned trace was divided into non-overlapping 2 s epochs (1000 samples at 500 Hz, giving a 0.5 Hz frequency resolution). Epochs were rejected in three stages, each restricted to the epochs surviving the stage(s) before it: (1) *running-speed gating* — an epoch was kept only if the animal's median running speed during it fell within 1–90 cm/s; speed was derived from a co-recorded tracking .csv (frame-to-frame x/y displacement divided by elapsed time, on the same UNIX clock as the LFP) and smoothed with a Savitzky–Golay filter (window 11 samples, polynomial order 3); (2) *delta/theta filter* — an epoch was rejected if its delta-band (1–3 Hz) power exceeded its theta-band (3–7 Hz) power, removing epochs dominated by slow drift or movement artifact rather than genuine theta; (3) *robust (MAD) outlier rejection* — an epoch was rejected if it was a high outlier (robust z > 5, median/MAD computed only from epochs surviving stages 1–2) on either broadband peak-to-peak amplitude or delta-band power.

**PSD estimation**: For each surviving epoch, a periodogram was computed with Welch's method (Hann window spanning the full epoch, no overlap, constant detrend), and the per-file PSD was taken as the mean periodogram across all epochs that passed the three-stage rejection above.

**Line-noise spectral interpolation**: Residual power around each mains harmonic (50, 100, 150, 200 Hz) was removed from the averaged PSD by linear interpolation across ±2 Hz windows centered on each harmonic (`fooof.utils.interpolate_spectrum`), correcting for artifacts the time-domain notch filter did not fully remove.

**Normalization**: Each file's PSD was converted to relative power by dividing by its own total power integrated between 1 and 100 Hz, so that spectra could be compared and pooled across files/animals independent of absolute signal amplitude.

**FOOOF/specparam fitting**: For each animal, the normalized PSDs of all its files were fit jointly with `FOOOFGroup` over a 1–40 Hz range, using aperiodic mode `'knee'` (to capture spectral curvature across this wide a range rather than assuming a single fixed slope), peak width limits of 1–8 Hz, a maximum of 6 peaks per spectrum, a minimum peak height of 0.1 (relative power units), and a peak threshold of 2 SD above the aperiodic fit. For each file this yielded aperiodic parameters (offset, knee, exponent), a set of periodic peak parameters (center frequency, power, bandwidth), and fit-quality metrics (r², model error).

**Theta peak extraction**: From each file's fitted peaks, the peak with the highest power whose center frequency fell within the theta band (3–7 Hz) was taken as that file's theta peak; the corresponding theta frequency range was defined as the peak's center frequency ± half its bandwidth. Files with no fitted peak in this range were marked as having no detectable theta peak.

**Fit-quality screening**: Files were flagged as poorly fit, and exported for separate inspection/exclusion, if their FOOOF r² fell below 0.98 or their model error exceeded 0.4.

**Plotting**: Group-level PSDs were plotted as the across-file mean ± SEM (relative power) per animal, and individual fits were visualized as the original spectrum, full FOOOF model, and aperiodic component overlaid, with the extracted theta frequency range shaded (`fooof.plts.spectra.plot_spectra_shading`).

#### fBOSC theta epoch characterization

Discrete theta oscillation episodes were detected directly from the continuous LFP using fBOSC, a FOOOF-informed extension of the BOSC/eBOSC oscillation-detection algorithm (Hughes et al., 2012; Kosciessa et al., 2020) that replaces BOSC's linear-regression background fit with a FOOOF aperiodic fit, allowing a statistically defensible power threshold to be set even where the background spectrum is not a straight line in log-log space (as is the case here across 1–40 Hz; see PSD/FOOOF methods above).

**Input signal**: fBOSC was run on the same per-file continuous LFP trace used for the PSD/FOOOF pipeline above (32 kHz → 500 Hz downsampled, 50/100/150/200 Hz notch-filtered, linearly detrended), but *without* the velocity, delta/theta, or MAD-outlier epoch rejection applied there — those criteria discard individual 2 s epochs from the PSD average, which would leave gaps unsuitable for the continuous, timestamp-preserving episode detection fBOSC performs.

**Time-frequency decomposition**: A Morlet wavelet transform (wavenumber = 8, i.e. the number of cycles trading off temporal versus frequency resolution) was applied to each file's full trace at frequencies from 1 to 48 Hz in 0.1 Hz steps, giving a time-frequency power matrix per file. One second of padding was retained on each side of the transform specifically to avoid convolution edge artifacts and trimmed before further analysis.

**Background fit and statistical power threshold**: For each file, the time-averaged log-power spectrum (edge padding excluded) was fit with FOOOF using the same settings as the PSD pipeline (knee aperiodic mode; peak width limits 1–8 Hz; up to 6 peaks; minimum peak height 0.1; peak threshold 2 SD). The resulting aperiodic (1/f) fit gave a file- and frequency-specific background power estimate, and the statistical power threshold at each frequency was set to the 95th percentile of a χ² distribution (2 degrees of freedom — the theoretical sampling distribution of wavelet power under a stationary background process) scaled by that background estimate, following the classic BOSC/eBOSC threshold definition.

**Frequency-range buffer**: Because the FOOOF background fit is constrained to exactly the range of frequencies scanned, an unconstrained knee fit right at the edge of that range tends to undershoot the true background curvature and understate the power threshold there — over-detecting spurious episodes at the top scanned frequency regardless of whether genuine oscillatory power exists there. To keep this edge artifact away from the 1–40 Hz band actually reported (shared with the FOOOF PSD analysis above), the scan was extended 8 Hz further, to 48 Hz, so the fit's own unconstrained edge falls outside the region of interest; episodes whose mean frequency fell at or above 40 Hz were discarded from the reported episode table after detection.

**Duration threshold**: An episode was additionally required to last at least dt(F) = max(3 cycles, 0.15 s × F) at its frequency F, rounded up to a whole cycle. Below the crossover frequency of 20 Hz (3 cycles ÷ 0.15 s), this reduces to the classic fixed 3-cycle BOSC criterion; above 20 Hz, a fixed cycle count alone would let increasingly brief (e.g. 30 ms at 100 Hz) noise transients pass purely because F is high, so the absolute 0.15 s floor was enforced in addition.

**Detection and episode assembly**: At each scanned frequency, time points whose wavelet power exceeded that frequency's power threshold were retained only where the resulting suprathreshold run lasted at least its duration threshold (BOSC_detect). Retained time-frequency points were first filtered for frequency-domain leakage (power required to exceed neighbouring frequency bins, scaled by the wavelet's own frequency bandwidth), then linked into continuous episodes by allowing the instantaneous frequency to step by at most one frequency bin between consecutive time samples (following the higher-power branch at points where detection could split in more than one direction), re-applying the duration criterion to the episode's mean frequency. Each resulting episode was summarized by its mean frequency, onset/offset time, duration (seconds, and cycles at the mean frequency), and mean wavelet power.

**Edge handling**: An optional FWHM-based post-processing step (deconvolving the temporal smearing that the wavelet's own extent introduces at episode onset/offset, then re-checking the duration criterion) is implemented in the pipeline but was not applied for the results reported here (postproc off). Independent of that setting, any detected samples falling within the 0.5 s padding reserved on each side of the detection step (to prevent the duration threshold itself from producing edge artifacts) were always trimmed from the final episode boundaries.

**Per-file processing and QC**: Episodes were detected separately for every .ncs file (i.e. per session/tetrode/channel), each with its own FOOOF background fit and power threshold, so thresholds adapt to that file's own noise floor and 1/f exponent. A QC figure (raw spectrum, full FOOOF model, aperiodic fit, and the resulting statistical power threshold, with any FOOOF-detected theta peak range shaded) was saved per file. The proportion of each file's recording occupied by theta episodes (summed episode duration ÷ total trace duration, an analogue of the classic Pepisode metric) was computed per file and compared across animals/days, alongside the pooled distribution of episode mean frequency and duration.

#### Bycycle analysis for theta symmetry characterization

Cycle-by-cycle waveform shape and burst structure of hippocampal theta were characterized in the time domain using bycycle (Cole and Voytek, 2019), following the approach and default parameters of the original bycycle theta-cycle tutorial, adapted to run per file on this pipeline's own cleaned LFP traces rather than the tutorial's example recordings.

**Input signal**: bycycle was run on the same per-file cleaned continuous LFP trace used for the fBOSC episode analysis above (32 kHz → 500 Hz downsampled, 50/100/150/200 Hz notch-filtered, linearly detrended) — i.e. without the epoch-level velocity, delta/theta, or MAD-outlier rejection applied for the PSD analysis, so that cycle detection operates on an unbroken trace.

**Per-animal theta band and FOOOF quality gating**: rather than scanning one fixed theta band for every animal, each animal's cycle-detection band was centred on that animal's own FOOOF-measured theta oscillation — the median FOOOF theta-peak centre frequency ± half the median peak bandwidth, taken across that animal's good-quality fits (a detected theta peak with r² ≥ 0.98 and error ≤ 0.4, the same bar used to flag poor FOOOF fits above); animals with no qualifying fit fell back to a fixed 3–7 Hz band. At the level of individual files, only files whose own FOOOF fit found a significant theta peak clearing this same quality bar were carried forward to bycycle — files with no frequency-domain evidence of a genuine oscillation were excluded, since cycle-by-cycle shape metrics are uninformative without one.

**Preprocessing**: matching the paper's own preprocessing (Cole and Voytek, 2019, `compute_shape()`), each retained trace was zero-phase low-pass filtered at 25 Hz (0.5 s FIR kernel) and then zero-phase high-pass filtered at 1 Hz (2 s FIR kernel) to remove slow drift, both applied without edge trimming.

**Cycle segmentation and feature extraction**: cycles were segmented trough-to-trough (`center_extrema = 'trough'`) using bycycle's `compute_features` (the pre-1.0, function-based API of the same vintage used in the original Cole & Voytek theta analysis script), which localizes each cycle's peak, trough, and the rise/decay zero-crossing midpoints — the time points at which voltage is halfway between the adjacent peak and trough voltage — and computes, per cycle: amplitude, period, rise–decay symmetry (the fraction of the period comprised of the rise phase), and peak–trough symmetry (the fraction of the period, bounded by consecutive rise midpoints rather than consecutive peaks, comprised of the peak phase).

**Burst detection**: a cycle was classified as part of a theta oscillatory burst if it and its neighbouring cycles cleared four criteria computed from these same per-cycle features: amplitude consistency > 0.6, period consistency > 0.75, and flank monotonicity > 0.6, with no additional amplitude-percentile cutoff (amplitude-fraction threshold = 0, rather than the tutorial's default 0.2, which was found to penalize genuine cycle-to-cycle amplitude modulation, e.g. from running speed or arousal, rather than screening non-oscillatory cycles). The first and last cycle of every file's trace were always excluded from bursts, and only runs of at least 3 consecutive qualifying cycles were retained as bursts (`min_n_cycles = 3`), matching the classic BOSC-style minimum burst duration.

**Robustness across burst-threshold choices**: following the robustness check of Cole and Voytek (2019), burst membership was additionally recomputed — at no extra fitting cost, since the four underlying per-cycle statistics above do not depend on threshold choice — under three further published burst-threshold parameter sets from that paper, for direct comparison against the thresholds above.

**Output**: every detected cycle (bursting or not) was retained as one row of a combined table, tagged with its animal, day, session, tetrode, and channel of origin, carrying per-cycle amplitude, period, rise–decay symmetry, and peak–trough symmetry, plus burst-membership labels under both the primary and the three robustness-check threshold sets, for downstream comparison of theta cycle shape/asymmetry across animals.

#### Autocorrelogram method to detect theta epochs and theta continuity

Continuity of hippocampal theta oscillations in the mole-rat LFP was quantified using the peak-range autocorrelogram (ACG) method of Dunn et al. (2022), adapted for our acquisition system and behavioural tracking.

**Signal preprocessing**: For each recording channel, the raw local field potential (Neuralynx .ncs, sampling rate 32 kHz) was resampled to 1 kHz to match the time base of the reference sinusoid bank (see below). 50 Hz mains noise and its harmonics (100, 150, 200 Hz) were removed with zero-phase IIR notch filters and Quality factor 30, the trace was linearly detrended, and a zero-phase 4th-order Butterworth low-pass filter with a 100 Hz cutoff was applied to exclude high-frequency content  that could otherwise distort the shape of the autocorrelogram used for theta frequency matching.

**Epoching and artifact rejection**: The cleaned trace was divided into non-overlapping 1 s epochs (1000 samples at 1kHz). Epochs were excluded in two stages: first, epochs whose Welch power spectral density showed greater power in the delta band (1–3 Hz) than the theta band (3–7 Hz) were discarded, removing segments dominated by slow drift or artifact that might otherwise pass an amplitude-based check as spuriously slow theta; second, among the remaining epochs, peak-to-peak amplitude was tested against a robust (median absolute deviation) outlier threshold (z > 5), with the reference median/MAD computed only from epochs surviving the first stage.

**Movement classification**: Animal position was tracked continuously and, for each session, x/y position was cleaned before use: frame-to-frame displacements implying an instantaneous speed above 90 cm/s was treated as tracking artifacts, removed, and linearly interpolated, and the resulting trace was Gaussian-smoothed (σ = 1 sample). Instantaneous running speed was derived from the displacement and elapsed time between consecutive cleaned position samples. Each LFP epoch was assigned the median tracked speed falling within its time window, aligned to the tracking data; epochs with median speed above 4 cm/s were labelled "moving" and the remainder "immobile".

**ACG peak-range quantification**: For each accepted epoch, the autocorrelogram was computed and normalised so its zero-lag value equalled 1. A bank of reference sinusoids spanning the theta range (3–7 Hz, 0.1 Hz steps) was constructed at the same sample rate and epoch length, and each was autocorrelated and normalised identically. For every data epoch, the normalised Euclidean distance (ED) between its autocorrelogram and each reference autocorrelogram was calculated (each reference distance itself normalised by that reference's own norm); the reference sinusoid giving the smallest distance (ED_min) was taken as the epoch's matched frequency estimate. The first side peak and its two flanking troughs were then located in the data autocorrelogram within search windows defined by the matched reference sinusoid's own peak/trough positions. Peak range was calculated as the peak height minus the mean of the two trough heights, and normalised by the matched reference sinusoid's own peak range — correcting for the fact that peak range shrinks with frequency for a pure sinusoid. A normalised peak range near 1 indicates a clean, sinusoidal (theta-like) oscillation; a value near 0 indicates a flat, non-oscillatory autocorrelogram.

**Fit-quality gating**: Epochs for which no reference sinusoid matched well (ED_min > 1.0) were excluded from quantitative comparison of peak range and frequency, since a poor fit indicates the epoch's autocorrelogram shape is not well characterised by any candidate sinusoid; these epochs were retained in the underlying dataset with a flag so that rejection rates could be reported and inspected separately.

**Aggregation**: Normalised peak range was compared between moving and immobile epochs within each channel and animal to test for the persistence of theta-band oscillatory structure during immobility, and matched-sinusoid frequency estimates were summarised per animal to characterise the dominant hippocampal theta frequency, following the analysis and figure conventions of Dunn et al. (2022, Fig. 4).

#### Theta depth profile

Depth-resolved theta characteristics were computed from Neuropixels 2.0 LFP recordings to relate theta power and phase to recording depth across the hippocampal formation, following the trace-stack and power/phase-profile conventions of Dunn et al. (2022, their Fig. 3; see Paper methods below).

**Preprocessing and motion correction**: Broadband LFP was loaded with SpikeInterface and preprocessed as: phase-shift correction for inter-channel sampling delays, automatic detection and removal of bad channels, a 1–500 Hz bandpass filter, and common median referencing across the probe (global reference). The result was resampled to 1000 Hz and cached to a memory-mapped binary file, so that the downstream motion-estimation step read a flat 1 kHz recording rather than repeatedly re-deriving the full preprocessing chain from the 30 kHz raw data (which was otherwise both slow and memory-intensive). Slow probe drift was estimated on this downsampled LFP using the DREDGE method for LFP traces (`dredge_lfp`, SpikeInterface), which estimates motion directly from a derived signal (0.5–250 Hz bandpass, phase-shifted, resampled to 250 Hz, second-order spatial derivative averaged across the probe's two columns) rather than from localised spikes; the resulting motion estimate was applied to the full-channel downsampled LFP by interpolation. The motion-corrected LFP was cached to disk for reuse.

**Channel depth ordering**: For each recording, surviving channels (post bad-channel removal) were ordered into a single top-to-bottom depth sequence spanning one or more probe depth ranges (in µm, read from the channel geometry) chosen to capture the dorsoventral extent of the hippocampal formation crossed by the probe track. The topmost channel of this sequence served as the depth-zero reference for the theta phase profile below.

**Noisy-epoch exclusion**: The whole recording was scanned in non-overlapping 1 s bins. For each bin, a robust broadband amplitude metric (median, across channels, of the per-channel SD) and the integrated theta-band (3–7 Hz) power per channel (Welch periodogram) were computed. A bin was flagged noisy if its amplitude metric exceeded median + 5 × 1.4826 × MAD of the metric across the recording (dead/zero-amplitude bins were also excluded); only bins surviving this filter contributed to the power and trace-stack profiles below.

**Theta power depth profile**: For each surviving channel, theta-band power (linear µV², integrated 3–7 Hz) was averaged across all clean bins over the entire recording and expressed in dB (10log10), giving one power-versus-depth profile per recording.

**Theta-filtered trace stacks**: Ten 5 s snippets were selected per recording by dividing the recording into ten equal-duration segments and, within each, choosing the least-noisy 5 s window free of flagged noisy bins (falling back to the least-noisy available window where none was fully clean), so that snippets were spread evenly through the session. Each snippet was zero-phase band-pass filtered to the theta band (3–7 Hz, 4th-order Butterworth, `sosfiltfilt`) on every channel and plotted as a depth-ordered stack of traces. A common amplitude gain, derived from the 99th percentile of the rectified theta amplitude pooled across all channels and snippets, was applied throughout so that trace amplitudes were directly comparable across depth and across snippets.

**Theta phase depth profile**: For each snippet, the first theta-band peak on the topmost (reference) channel defined the phase origin (0°); on every other channel, the first peak at or after this reference time gave that channel's lag, converted to phase as 2π × (lag mod T)/T, where T is the reference channel's own median inter-peak interval (theta period) within that snippet. Peaks were required to exceed a prominence of 0.5 trace SDs to reject sub-threshold ripples that would otherwise corrupt period/lag estimation. Per-channel phases were combined across the ten snippets by circular mean, giving a mean phase (reported signed, in (−180°, +180°], so channels leading the reference show a negative phase/lag) and a resultant length (circular concentration, 0–1) reflecting cross-snippet consistency; the theta period was likewise averaged across snippets and used to convert the phase axis to a lag in milliseconds.

### Spike theta coupling using pass index method

Analysis was carried out per isolated unit (tetrode spike file), using the LFP channel  recorded on the same or closest neighbour tetrode as the theta reference. LFP signal for tetrode 3, tetrode 4, tetrode 5, and tetrode 6 was noisey hence the LFP signal on their nearest neighbours tetrode 2, tetrode 1, tetrode 7, and tetrode 8, respectively were used to correlate spiking activity with clean LFP signal. Instantaneous theta phase was estimated by zero-phase bandpass filtering the raw LFP into the theta band (3–7 Hz; 4th-order Butterworth, second-order-sections form applied with `sosfiltfilt` to avoid the numerical instability of a transfer-function/pole representation at narrow or near-DC bands) and taking the argument of its *Use Andre's wavelet method here insted* Hilbert transform. The unwrapped phase was interpolated to each spike time by linearly interpolating the cosine and sine of the unwrapped phase separately (not the angle itself), which avoids the discontinuity that linear interpolation of a wrapped angle would otherwise introduce at the 0/2π boundary; spikes falling outside the LFP's recorded time range were clamped to the nearest edge sample.

**Theta phase-locking and Theta Modulation Index (TMI; Frank et al., 2001)**: For each unit with at least 8 spikes with LFP coverage, the resultant vector of per-spike theta phases was used to compute the mean resultant length (MRL), preferred phase, and a Rayleigh test of non-uniformity (reported for reference/QC alongside the shuffle-based test below). Independently, a Theta Modulation Index (TMI) was computed following Frank et al. (2001): per-spike phases were tiled across five repeated 360° cycles, binned into 36°-wide bins, and the resulting histogram was Gaussian-smoothed (7-point kernel, σ = 0.5 bins, edge-padded convolution); the middle two cycles (360–1080°, wrapped back to 0–360°) were retained and max-normalized, and TMI was defined as 1 minus the trough of this normalized histogram (TMI → 1 for strongly theta-modulated firing, → 0 for phase-uniform firing). The phase bins of peak and trough firing were read from this same smoothed histogram for consistency with the reported TMI.

Significance of the TMI was assessed with the shuffling procedure of Frank et al. (2001), rather than by Rayleigh statistics alone, since TMI captures modulation depth (including bimodal/non-Rayleigh profiles) rather than only unimodal phase concentration. On each of 1000 shuffles, every spike was reassigned an independent random phase drawn uniformly from [0°, 360°), except that consecutive spikes (in time order) less than 50 ms apart and falling within the same 36°-wide bin of their *real* theta phase were grouped into a single burst beforehand and assigned one shared random phase, preserving burst structure while destroying true phase-locking; the TMI of each shuffled phase set was then recomputed identically to the observed value. The shuffle p-value was the (add-one-smoothed) proportion of surrogate TMIs greater than or equal to the observed TMI, and a unit was classified as significantly theta-modulated (TMI_Significant) at p < 0.05.

**Phase precession via the Pass Index method (Climer, Newman & Hasselmo, 2013)**: Only units that passed the TMI shuffle test above were carried forward to test for phase precession, since the Pass Index method presupposes a theta-rhythmic spatial signal. An occupancy-normalized 2D rate map was built (4-cm bins for place cells; nearest-position occupancy filled by Euclidean distance transform before smoothing), smoothed with a Gaussian kernel (σ = smoothing width / bin size / 2, width = 3 × bin size), and normalized into a 0–1 "field index" map — min–max normalized for place cells. The animal's trajectory was resampled at evenly spaced steps along its cumulative arc length (matching the number of original tracking samples), and the field index was read off along this resampled path, then band-pass filtered along the arc-length dimension with a zero-phase Butterworth filter: for place cells, the pass-band was set automatically from the cell's own field size (radius r estimated from the area exceeding 20% of peak rate, band = [1/(6r), 3/r] cycles per unit distance). The Hilbert transform of this filtered field-index trace gave an instantaneous "pass index" running continuously from −1 (start of a field pass) through 0 (center) to +1 (end of pass), which was then interpolated (nearest-neighbor on the unwrapped phase) to each spike time. Theta phase at each spike, for this step, was computed the same way as the theta phase-locking analysis above (3–7 Hz Butterworth, Hilbert transform).

**Circular–linear regression (Kempter et al., 2012)**: The relationship between each unit's spike pass-index and its instantaneous theta phase was fit with the circular–linear regression of Kempter et al. (2012): the slope s (cycles of theta phase per unit of pass index) and intercept b were chosen to maximize the mean resultant length of (theta phase − 2πs·pass-index). Because this objective is highly multimodal in s. The circular–linear correlation coefficient ρ and its significance were then computed from the fitted phase residuals following Kempter et al. (2012) (z-transform of ρ referenced to a normal approximation). Only units with at least 50 spikes falling within the region of overlap between tracking and LFP coverage were fit.

A unit's total phase-precession slope was expressed in degrees of theta phase swept per full field pass (accounting for the pass index spanning two units, −1 to +1, i.e. slope_deg_per_pass = rad2deg(4π·s)). A significant fit (p < 0.05) was classified as phase-**precessing** if its slope fell between −1440 and −22 deg/pass (phase advances to earlier theta phase across the pass) and phase-**recessing** if its slope fell between +22 and +1440 deg/pass (phase progressively delayed across the pass), rather than only reporting the classically expected negative-slope precession. Fit quality was summarized as r² (ρ²) and as the total phase range actually swept by the cell's spikes (|slope| × the observed extent of pass index, rather than the full −1 to +1 range, since a given cell's spikes may not cover an entire pass).

**Arena comparison (Circle vs. Linear vs. Open field)**: For sessions organized as `<animal>/<arena>/DayN/<session>`, cells classified as significantly precessing or recessing were pooled across animals within each arena and compared on r², |slope| (deg/pass), and phase range (deg) using a Kruskal–Wallis omnibus test per metric followed by pairwise Mann–Whitney U tests between arenas. The same omnibus/pairwise approach was applied to TMI values from all significantly theta-modulated cells (independent of precession status), pooled by arena, to test whether theta-phase-locking strength itself differed across arena geometries.

**Autocorrelation metric of oscillatory activity**: The LFP was first high-pass filtered using IIR (Infinite Impulse Response) filters (rat: 6th-order high-pass Chebyshev Type II filter with 80 dB of stopband attenuation and a passband edge frequency of 2 Hz; ferret: 4th-order high-pass Chebyshev Type II filter with 80 dB of stopband attenuation and a passband edge frequency of 1 Hz) and 50 Hz notch filtered (14th-order band-stop Chebyshev Type II filter with 60 dB of stopband attenuation, pass-band edge frequencies of 48 and 52 Hz and stop-band frequencies of 49 and 51 Hz). The signal was then segmented into one-second epochs and autocorrelograms were calculated for each epoch. For each autocorrelogram, the Euclidean distance (ED) to the autocorrelations of sine waves of varying frequency (4-14 Hz for the rat, 2–14 Hz for the ferret, 0.1 Hz increments) was calculated. The ED between the data and the sine autocorrelograms was normalised by the ED of the individual ED of each sine autocorrelogram. The ‘matched’ sine autocorrelogram (i.e. with the minimum Euclidean distance from the data epoch autocorrelogram) was used to identify the first autocorrelogram peak. The range of this peak was measured from the maxima of the first peak to the average of the two surrounding troughs. This peak range measurement was then normalised by the peak range of the matched sine, as the peak range was found to vary as a function of frequency. The frequency of the signal in the data epoch was estimated as the frequency of the sine wave that was used to calculate the matched sine autocorrelogram.
The head speed signal was also segmented into corresponding 1 s epochs, for which the mean speed was found for each epoch. For comparison of moving vs. immobile epochs (Figs. 4, 5) conservative speed thresholds were chosen to ensure good separation of locomotor contingencies (moving vs. immobile) given that speeds were averaged over a one second window.

**Field index**: Calculation of a measure of how in field the animal was, or the ‘field index’, started by calculating the occupancy-normalized firing rate for 1 × 1-cm bins of position data. Data were then smoothed by a two-dimensional (2D) convolution with a pseudo-Gaussian kernel with a five-pixel (5 cm) standard deviation (Fig. 3E). ## Bycycle; Cole Voytek 2017 et.al.

**Theta cycle analysis**:  The presence and features of hippocampal theta oscillations were analyzed using our previously described cycle-by-cycle analysis approach ​(Cole and Voytek, 2018)​. Briefly, a broad bandpass filter (1-25 Hz) was applied and then peaks and troughs were localized (Figure 1A, dots) in order to segment the signal into theta (4-10 Hz) cycles. Note this broad bandpass filter did not substantially affect the theta oscillation asymmetry of interest (compare gray and black traces in Figure 1A). A peak-to-peak segmentation was chosen because spiking was concentrated around the trough (Figure 4D,E) and so bursts of spiking around the trough would be analyzed in a single cycle (rather than 2 cycles if a trough-to-trough segmentation was used). For each cycle, four features were computed as shown in Figure 1B: amplitude, period, rise-decay symmetry, and peak-trough symmetry. Rise and decay midpoints were defined as the time points at which the voltage was halfway between the adjacent peak and trough voltages. These midpoints were used to represent the boundaries between peak and trough segments. Rise-decay symmetry is defined as the fraction of the period that is comprised of the rise phase. Peak-trough symmetry is similarly defined as the fraction of the period comprised of the peak phase, but the period in this case is bounded by consecutive rise midpoints instead of consecutive peaks.  It is important to appreciate that the neural oscillations are not present during the entire recording ​(Feingold et al., 2015; Jones, 2016; Lundqvist et al., 2016)​. Therefore, it is useful to determine the segments of the signal in which the oscillation is present because measuring theta features of a signal segment without a prominent theta oscillation will add noise to the analysis ​(Cole and Voytek, 2018)​. Therefore, only cycles that are determined to be part of a theta oscillatory burst were analyzed. However, the task of identifying the segments of the signal with oscillatory components is challenging and currently unsolved ​(Kosciessa et al., 2018)​. It is unclear if there are discrete times in which an oscillator is on and off, so perhaps there is no objective solution.  The approach for burst detection has been thoroughly described previously ​(Cole and Voytek, 2018)​, but briefly, a segment (cycle) of the signal was determined to be part of an oscillatory burst if its amplitude and period were comparable to adjacent cycles, and if its rise and decay flanks were mainly monotonic. Like with any burst detection algorithm, it relies on thresholds that must be semi-arbitrarily defined ​(Feingold et al., 2015; Hughes et al., 2012)​. In order to address this limitation, we ran our analysis with a range of burst detection parameters to assure that results were not simply dependent on one specific choice of settings. For the results shown in the main paper, the parameters were chosen as those that optimized the F1 score (equally weighted precision and recall) of a simulated signal with a signal-to-noise ratio that appears roughly similar to the hippocampal theta rhythm ​(Cole and Voytek, 2018)​. Thresholds were set such that adjacent cycles’ amplitudes and periods could be no more than 60% and 45% different, respectively, and the cycle flanks must be at least 80% monotonic. With these settings, theta oscillations were detected to be present 50-85% of the time across recordings.
